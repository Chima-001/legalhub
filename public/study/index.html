01 / READING

Brief a case in six lines.

Build a note you can use to answer a question later, not a shorter copy of the judgment. Start with the order the court made and work backwards.

Citation
Write the case name, court, date and source. Leave a space for the relevant page or paragraph.
Material facts
Keep the facts that affected the decision. Ask: would changing this fact change the answer?
Issue
Write one question the court had to decide. Separate it from the parties’ broader disagreement.
Holding
Answer that question in one sentence. Identify what the court actually ordered.
Reasoning
State the rule and how the court connected it to the material facts. Separate the necessary reasoning (ratio) from observations not needed to reach the result (obiter).
Limit
Name a different fact or procedural setting that could distinguish a later case.
Try it Which sentence belongs in the ratio?

Fictional exercise. A panel decides a notice was late because the rule requires receipt by noon and the notice arrived at 12:15. A member adds that email would be more convenient next year.

The receipt deadline and its application explain the outcome. The suggestion about email does not. If your note treats the suggestion as a binding rule, revise it.

02 / WRITING

Put the work in the application.

Use issue, rule, application and conclusion as a working structure. The longest part usually answers “why does this fact satisfy, or fail to satisfy, this element?”

  1. Frame the issue. Ask about a particular party, act and consequence. Split independent issues rather than giving one global answer.
  2. State the rule. Give its elements, a reliable authority and any relevant exception. Do not invent a citation when you cannot recall it.
  3. Test both readings. Pair each element with a fact. Identify the strongest contrary inference and say which additional fact would resolve it.
  4. Conclude at the right strength. “Likely, if…” can be more accurate than a categorical answer where the record is incomplete.
Try it Dispatch or receipt?

Using the fictional noon rule above, a sender posts a notice at 11:40. The problem says nothing about arrival. Can you conclude the notice was on time?

No. Dispatch alone does not establish receipt. Explain why the arrival time matters and give the result under each possible finding.

03 / RESEARCH

Check the authority before relying on it.

A convincing quotation is not enough. Keep a short verification trail next to each proposition in your notes.

  1. Identify the source. Is it legislation, a judgment, a textbook or commentary? A headnote or lecture slide is a starting point, not the court’s reasons.
  2. Match the jurisdiction and date. For a Nigerian-law question, check which legislature, court and time period matter. A familiar title alone does not settle applicability.
  3. Read the passage in context. Record the section, subsection, page or paragraph. Check whether the proposition is a submission, a dissent, or part of the decision.
  4. Check current status online. Look for amendments, commencement dates and later treatment in an authoritative source. Mark an unchecked proposition as “verify” rather than presenting it as current law.
Copy to your notebook A source-check line

Proposition → source & court/legislature → pinpoint → relevant date → later treatment → checked on ____.

This is a study method, not legal advice or a live authority database. No legislation or case status is updated while you are offline.
